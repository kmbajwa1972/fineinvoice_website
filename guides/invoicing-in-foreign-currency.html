How to Invoice a Client in a Foreign Currency

Generated by FineInvoice.com · Updated October 2026

Invoicing a client in another country raises questions a local invoice never does: which currency should the amount be in, what exchange rate applies, and who pays the conversion fees? Settling these before you send the invoice prevents most payment disputes.

Which currency should you bill in?

OptionGood for youWatch out for
Your own currencyNo conversion on your side, predictable incomeClient carries the exchange risk and may push back
Client's currencyEasier for the client to approve and payYou carry the exchange risk and conversion fees
A common currency (USD, EUR, GBP)Neutral, widely supported by payment servicesBoth sides may still convert at some point

For long projects, billing in your own currency or a stable common one protects you from rate swings between the quote and the payment.

Decide which exchange rate applies

Rates move daily. Agree up front whether the amount is fixed in the invoice currency (the simplest option) or converted at the rate on the payment date. Write the rule on the invoice, for example: "Amount due is fixed at EUR 920.00. Payment must arrive in full."

Plan for fees

Money sent across borders can lose value in several places: your bank or payment service fee, the client's bank fee, an intermediary bank, and the exchange margin. Many people forget this and receive less than they invoiced. Decide whether to add a small buffer to your price, and say clearly who pays transfer fees.

What to put on the invoice

  • The currency code next to every total (USD, EUR, GBP, AED, PKR)
  • A single currency for the whole invoice, not mixed lines
  • Optionally, an informational equivalent in the other currency, labelled as an estimate
  • Payment details that suit the currency, such as an IBAN or SWIFT code for international transfers
  • The due date and any late-payment terms

Worked example and calculator

You price a project at 1,000 in your currency and your client wants the invoice in euros. If 1 unit of your currency is worth 0.92 euros, the invoice is EUR 920.00. If the payment route takes 2.5% in fees, you receive about 897.00 euros, which converts back to roughly 975.00 in your currency. The rate here is only an illustration, not a quote.

Tips that save money and arguments

  • Quote the invoice in the currency the client will actually pay in
  • Mention that the rate is fixed, or for how long the quote is valid
  • Choose a payment route with transparent fees, and compare the real rate with the mid-market rate
  • Keep a record of the rate and date you used for your own bookkeeping

Frequently asked questions

Can I show two currencies on one invoice?

You can show a second currency as an estimate, but keep one legally binding total in a single currency.

Who should pay the transfer fees?

Whoever you agree on. If you say nothing, you will usually end up absorbing them, so state it in your terms.

Does tax change for foreign clients?

Often yes. VAT, GST and sales tax rules for overseas clients differ by country and by type of service. Check your tax authority or an accountant.
