What to Include on an Invoice: A Practical Checklist

The exact invoice requirements can vary by country, industry, and tax status, but most professional invoices share the same core information. A clear layout helps customers understand the charge and gives you a useful record for your own business.

Seller information

Show the name of the person or business issuing the invoice. Add contact details such as an email address, phone number, website, or business address when appropriate. If local rules require tax registration information, include the required details.

If customers know you by a trading name, make the relationship with the legal or registered business clear where required. Consistent seller information also helps customers recognize legitimate invoices and reduces the chance of payment being delayed because the recipient is unsure who issued the document.

Customer information

Identify the customer receiving the invoice. For a business customer, this may include the company name, contact person, billing address, and any purchase order or project reference supplied by the customer.

For an individual customer, include only the information that is appropriate and necessary for the transaction. Check spelling and contact details before sending. A small error in the customer name can make a document harder to match with an order or payment.

Invoice number and dates

Give every invoice a unique reference. Include the invoice date and, when relevant, the payment due date. For recurring work, clear dates make it easier to see which period the invoice covers.

Your numbering system can be simple, such as INV-1001, INV-1002, and INV-1003. What matters most is that references are consistent and that you can locate the related document later. If local rules prescribe a numbering method, follow those rules instead.

Line items

Describe each product or service in a separate line. Include quantity, unit price or rate, and line total where useful. Avoid vague descriptions when a short, specific description would help the customer understand the charge.

For services, explain the work in terms the customer can recognize. For products, use names or descriptions that match the order. If a project includes several stages, separate them so the customer can see how the total was calculated.

Service period or delivery details

Some invoices benefit from showing the period covered by the work, delivery date, project stage, or another reference to the underlying transaction. This is especially useful for recurring services because the customer can immediately see which month, week, milestone, or order the invoice relates to.

Subtotal, taxes, and discounts

Show the subtotal before adjustments. If you apply a discount, make it visible. If tax applies, show the applicable tax rate or amount in accordance with the rules that apply to your business.

Do not assume the same tax treatment applies to every customer or location. Tax obligations can vary according to jurisdiction, registration status, product or service, and customer location. This checklist is general information and is not a substitute for professional tax advice.

Total amount due

Make the final amount prominent. If the customer has already paid part of the invoice, show the amount paid and remaining balance so the amount still due is clear.

If the invoice is issued in a foreign currency, make the currency clear near the totals. Avoid presenting a number without a currency symbol or code when the customer could reasonably misunderstand the amount.

Payment instructions

Explain how the customer can pay. This might include bank transfer details, a payment link, card instructions, or another payment method. Make sure the instructions are current before sending the invoice.

If payment is by bank transfer, check the account information carefully. If you use a payment link, test that the link works. If you accept multiple methods, put the most useful option first and keep the instructions short enough to scan quickly.

Payment terms

State when payment is expected if the transaction is not due immediately. You can use a specific due date or another clear term agreed with the customer. If your business uses late-payment terms, state them clearly and make sure they are appropriate for the rules that apply to you.

Optional notes

You can add a short thank-you message, project note, delivery information, late-payment terms, or other useful instructions. Keep optional notes relevant and easy to scan. The main purpose of the invoice is to communicate the transaction and amount due clearly.

Final review checklist

  • Correct customer and seller details
  • Unique invoice number
  • Correct invoice and due dates
  • Accurate descriptions and quantities
  • Correct calculations
  • Applicable tax and discount information
  • Clear currency and total
  • Clear balance due
  • Working payment instructions
  • Relevant notes or project references

Why a clear invoice is useful

A clear invoice saves time for both sides. Customers can see what they are paying for without requesting clarification, while the seller has a consistent record that can be matched to payments and future questions.

It is usually better to include the information a customer actually needs than to fill the page with unnecessary text. A well-structured invoice should be easy to scan on a computer or phone and should remain understandable when printed or saved as a PDF.
