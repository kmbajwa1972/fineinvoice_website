How to Invoice International Clients: Currency, Fees and Taxes

Working with a client in another country can open new opportunities, but cross-border billing involves a few decisions that may not come up with a local customer. You need to agree on the currency, show the correct customer and supplier details, choose a practical way to receive payment, and check whether any tax or electronic-invoicing rules apply.

The key is to agree on these details before work starts instead of trying to settle them after you send the bill. This guide offers a practical workflow for freelancers and small businesses. International tax rules differ by country, the type of customer, and whether you supply goods or services, so treat the examples as general guidance rather than a determination of your tax obligations.

1. Agree on the currency before quoting

Ask which currency the client expects to see on the quote and invoice. A client may prefer USD, EUR, GBP, or its local currency, while you may manage your records in a different currency. Decide on one billing currency for the project where possible, and record the agreement in the proposal, contract, or order confirmation.

Consider your costs and the risk of exchange-rate changes. If your costs are in one currency but the client pays in another, the amount you receive after conversion can change. For a long project, agree whether the price remains fixed for the entire engagement or can be reviewed at defined milestones.

Example of clear quote wording

“The service fee for this project is USD 1,200. The invoice will be issued in USD, and payment is due within 15 calendar days of the invoice date. Any bank or intermediary fees are handled as stated in our written agreement.”

This example is a commercial arrangement, not a rule that suits every country or customer. Make sure the currency and fee terms are accepted by both sides and are consistent with the contract.

2. Show currency and amounts clearly

Do not rely on a bare symbol when a reader could mistake one currency for another. Use a clear code such as USD, EUR, GBP, or PKR alongside the amount, especially when you work with several countries. Keep the currency consistent across line items, tax amounts, totals, and payment instructions.

If you and the customer agree to show an approximate amount in a second currency for convenience, label it as an estimate and identify the exchange-rate date or source used. State which currency is actually payable. Avoid presenting an indicative conversion as if it were a guaranteed settlement amount.

Example invoice summary
  • Website maintenance: USD 800.00
  • Agreed discount: USD 50.00
  • Subtotal before any applicable tax: USD 750.00
  • Total due: USD 750.00

This is a simple illustration only. Add any tax that is actually required and use the calculation and display rules that apply to your transaction.

3. Understand exchange rates and conversion records

Your invoice currency, the currency your bank receives, and the currency used in your accounting or tax records may be different. Ask your bank or payment provider how it converts incoming payments, which exchange rate it applies, and whether it adds a conversion margin or separate fee.

When you must record a foreign-currency transaction in your local accounting currency, use the conversion method and date required by the relevant accounting or tax rules. Keep a record of the original invoice amount, the rate and date used for your records, and the amount actually received. Do not assume that the rate shown by a search engine, a bank, and a payment provider will be identical.

For example, an invoice for EUR 1,000 might reach your bank after a provider conversion and fees. The invoice still documents what the client owed in EUR; your accounting entry should separately reflect the amount and conversion required by the rules that apply to you. The example does not prescribe a particular exchange rate or accounting treatment.

4. Decide who pays transfer and processing fees

International payments can involve fees charged by the sending bank, receiving bank, intermediary banks, a card processor, or a payment platform. Some charges are deducted before the funds arrive, so the amount received may be lower than the invoice total even when the client sent the stated amount.

Before sending the invoice, agree on how fees will be handled and check whether your proposed wording is permitted in the countries involved. If the client is expected to cover bank charges, say so in the agreement and payment instructions. If you absorb the charges, account for them when setting your price. Never add a surprise fee after the client has accepted the price.

Offer payment methods that are actually available to both parties. Confirm the recipient country, supported currencies, settlement timing, and withdrawal options with the provider before relying on it for a project.

5. Check tax and registration rules before issuing the invoice

Cross-border tax treatment is not determined by the customer's country alone. It can depend on where you are established, whether the customer is a business or an individual, what you sell, where goods are delivered or services are treated as supplied, and whether either party has a relevant tax registration.

Before issuing the invoice, check whether you need to include a tax registration number, charge tax, apply a reverse-charge or other treatment, show a required statement, or use a prescribed invoice format. Goods shipped across borders may also raise customs, import-tax, and delivery-document questions that do not apply in the same way to remote services.

  • Identify whether the customer is a business or an individual.
  • Confirm the customer's legal name, billing address, and any tax number required for the transaction.
  • Check the rules for the country where you operate and the destination or customer's location.
  • Determine whether the supply is goods, digital services, consulting, or another type of service.
  • Verify any required tax wording, tax breakdown, invoice numbering, or e-invoicing format.
Important: never mark a transaction “tax-free,” “zero-rated,” or “reverse charge” just because the customer is overseas. Use that treatment only when it applies to the specific transaction and you have checked the relevant requirements.

6. Include the information the client needs

International clients may have their own accounts-payable checks. A clear invoice helps them match the charge to the agreed work and submit it for payment without repeated questions. Ask whether the client requires a purchase-order number, project code, vendor number, portal submission, or a specific invoice format.

As a starting point, check that the invoice includes the following items where applicable:

  • Your business or legal name and contact details, as required.
  • The client's correct legal or billing name and address.
  • A unique invoice number, issue date, and relevant supply or service date.
  • A clear description of the work or goods, with quantities or milestones where useful.
  • Line amounts, discounts, applicable tax details, and a clearly identified total and currency.
  • The agreed due date, accepted payment method, and any reference the customer must include.
  • Tax or registration details and required statements for the relevant jurisdiction, if applicable.

This is a practical checklist, not a universal legal list. Official invoice requirements can vary, and some transactions require extra information or a particular electronic format.

7. Make payment instructions specific and safe

Tell the client exactly how to pay and what reference to include. If you accept a bank transfer, provide the correct beneficiary name and the required bank details through a suitable channel. If you use a payment link, test it and make sure it leads to the expected payment page. Confirm who is responsible for conversion and transfer charges.

Be cautious about requests to change bank details by email. If either side receives an unexpected change request, verify it through a previously established contact method before sending money. Keep payment instructions consistent across the contract, invoice, and follow-up messages.

8. Set a due date that both sides understand

Terms such as “Net 15” may be familiar to some businesses but unclear to others. State the actual due date when possible and clarify how the date is calculated, including whether the period uses calendar days or business days. If the customer's accounts-payable process requires invoice approval, confirm what starts the payment period.

Example payment wording

“Payment is due by 15 November 2026. Please quote invoice INT-2026-014 as the payment reference. The agreed invoice currency is USD. Please contact us before the due date if your accounts-payable team needs a purchase-order number or supporting document.”

Replace the example date and reference with the actual details for your transaction. Make sure the due date matches the agreed contract and applicable rules.

9. Keep a record from quote to payment

Save the accepted quote or contract, the invoice PDF, the exchange-rate information used for your records, payment-provider statements, bank fees, and confirmation of the amount received. Use the invoice number to connect the quote, invoice, payment, and any later credit or correction document.

If the customer pays less than the invoice amount, first compare the payment confirmation and fees with the agreement. Do not automatically assume that the customer underpaid or that the bank made an error. Ask for the transfer details and reconcile the difference before sending a balance reminder.

10. Final checklist for international invoices

  • Billing currency and price are agreed in writing.
  • The currency code is shown clearly throughout the invoice.
  • The customer name, address, purchase-order details, and tax information are correct.
  • The tax treatment and any cross-border invoicing requirements have been checked.
  • Transfer fees, conversion, and payment method are understood by both sides.
  • The invoice has a unique number, clear due date, and complete payment instructions.
  • Supporting records are saved so the payment can be reconciled later.
